Mahjong you can open at your desk

Keeping a game open on a work machine is fussier than it sounds. You cannot install anything, an account means records piling up somewhere, and the screen looks like a game at a glance. Ippatsu answers those three separately: it opens in a tab, it has no accounts at all, and you can change what the screen looks like.

Open it in your browser

No account, and the game sets no cookies and collects nothing that identifies you

Opening the address in a browser is the whole of it. There is no executable to download, no sign-up screen and no login screen. An account is not optional here; there is none. With nowhere to log in, there is also no server holding your progress for you.

The game does not use cookies and collects nothing that identifies you. It never asks for a name or an email address. There is no screen that could.

There is one thing it does collect. A visit counter runs and logs a line for which screen opened and for a few outcomes: entries such as "skin/excel" when you change the screen theme, "round/win-ron" when a hand ends, or "match/place-3" when a match does. Your tiles, your replays and your save file are not sent. The game sets no cookies. That said, a request is a request: connection details such as your IP address reach the counting service. The game does not store that separately or attach it to a name. The page you are reading carries the same script, and it is the only script loaded from outside: no ads, no other trackers.

There is exactly one place your progress accumulates. Cleared stages, match records, settings and replays all stay inside the browser you are using right now. With no account, no server ends up with a history under your name, and no login notification lands in your work inbox.

  • There is no sign-up or login screen.
  • Progress is kept only inside the browser.
  • Nothing to install. It opens in a browser tab.
  • Moving to another device means exporting your save to a file and importing that file yourself.

That last line is what the arrangement costs you. Progress made at work does not follow you home on its own. To move it you export the save to a file and import it on the other device. Tedious, but it is the honest flip side: anything that follows you automatically is already stored somewhere.

The reason this is comfortable to leave open at work is really this rather than the disguise. What a screen looks like is a matter of who is looking; what is and is not collected is something you can check.

Sound is off by default

Music, sound effects and NPC voices are three separate switches, and all three are off the first time you open the game. Not "you can mute it": until you turn one on, this game makes no sound. Nobody can promise a browser tab will stay silent whatever happens, but this one does not make the first noise.

Three screen themes: the look changes, and so does the wording

Under "Screen Theme" in the settings there are three built-in disguise themes besides the plain one. It is not only paint: the words on screen change into that tool's vocabulary as well. The four screenshots below are real screens: the title screen of each theme, plus one match in progress under the spreadsheet theme. They were captured with the Korean interface, so the labels in them are in Korean; the English interface puts the English words listed below in the same slots. Incidentally, what switches the theme is the sheet tabs along the bottom under the spreadsheet, the "Edition" links at the top right of the masthead under News, and a status-bar entry under the code editor. They are one click each, this being the title screen. The language switcher hides in the same strip, except under News, where it sits at the top left of the masthead instead.

Spreadsheet

It imitates the start page of a spreadsheet program: a green sidebar and search box on the left, a template gallery and recent-file list in the middle, a summary card of totals and averages on the right, sheet tabs along the bottom. Inside a match the words follow. Riichi is "Finalize", ron is "Complete", East, South, West and North become Col A, Col B, Col C and Col D, and hands are counted in "Sheets".

The spreadsheet stays up during the match. A formula bar and column headers run across the top, small side tables of validation rules, notes and a pivot summary sit left and right, and the status bar at the bottom shows a sum and an average. The table is a single card in the middle of all that, and your hand is fourteen cells under green headers. Even the prompt on your turn reads "Select a cell to finalize": choose a tile to discard.

News

A newspaper front page: masthead in the center, issue number and section tabs beneath it, a ticker of market indices, a lead story on the left and a most-read list on the right. Here riichi is "Publish" and ron is "File", East, South, West and North are Politics, Economy, Society and World, and hands are counted in "Issues".

Code editor

The welcome tab of a code editor, down to the activity bar along the far left, three columns of start actions, recently opened paths and walkthroughs, an editor tab with source open, a terminal panel printing test results below it, and a status bar carrying a branch name and a problem count. Riichi is "commit" and chii is "import"; East, South, West and North are src, lib, test and dist. Hands are counted in "Builds".

The renaming is not confined to a couple of spots. Hand numbers, the word for the dealer, the names of the calls (chii, pon, kan), riichi and ron, the count of tiles left, the heading on the settlement screen: each theme has its own set. So mahjong vocabulary and tool vocabulary rarely end up side by side inside a match. Outside the match screen there are two exceptions, and they are in "In fairness" below.

Only the names differ; the actions are identical. Press "Finalize" and you have declared riichi. Press "commit" and the same thing happens.

It is still a game

The themes live in the presentation layer only. Whichever one is on, rule adjudication and scoring are exactly the same. The design document pins the skins as purely presentational, and the rules engine does not know which theme is running.

So what you pick up here carries over. What you learned as "Finalize" is riichi, and what you read as "Col A" is East. Swap the words back and the same judgments hold at any other mahjong table.

Mahjong is easy to leave open during a workday because of the genre itself. It is turn-based, so it asks nothing of your reflexes, and a move is one decision. A theme does not shrink the match either: same rules, same scoring, same opponents. You choose the length before you start: four hands East-only, eight for a hanchan. If the leader is short of 30,000 points at the final hand it does not stop there but runs on until the result is settled: at most one more wind, so eight hands at most East-only and twelve at most for a hanchan.

In fairness

Overselling a disguise is something a first-time user spots immediately. Two things to know up front.

There is no hotkey that flips the screen theme on or off. That is deliberate, not an oversight: no key does anything to the theme. You pick it from the theme switcher on the title screen or from the settings, and it stays picked the next time you open the game. If you were hoping for a panic key, this game does not have one.

Two screens do not follow the theme. The first is the beginner lessons and the diagnostic quiz: start the tutorial with a theme on and the ordinary mahjong tiles come up there anyway. That is the first path a new player walks, so it is worth knowing in advance. The second is the rulebook and the position analyzer, which open in a new tab in plain mahjong notation. Inside the game the two entry points are relabeled to the neutral "Reference Doc" and "Review Tool", and hovering either button shows "Opens in a new tab", but once that tab opens, that is as far as it goes. Read the disguise as reaching the match screen and no further.

If you are new to mahjong

If you have never played, the rules are the faster starting point. It is also worth finishing one hand on the plain screen before turning a theme on: the disguised words only read if you know what they replaced. What to learn first, and how far you can get on your own, is written up separately.
